Chapter 2

Kinematics & Coordinate Frames

Imagine you move your shoulder and elbow in specific ways — you can predict exactly where your fingertip ends up. Now flip the question: given a point you want to touch, how do you angle your shoulder, elbow, and wrist to get your finger there? Those two questions — joints → tip position and tip position → joints — are exactly what this chapter teaches a robot to answer. Along the way we introduce the mathematical tools that make it possible. The math may look intimidating at first, but every formula has a plain-English meaning explained right next to it.
New terms in this chapter — read these first
Kinematics
The study of how something moves, without worrying about the forces involved. For a robot arm: given the joint angles, where does the tip end up?
Coordinate frame
A set of three perpendicular axes (x, y, z) attached to a specific point — like a tiny 3D compass stuck to each link. All measurements are made relative to some frame.
Forward kinematics (FK)
Computing the tip's position and orientation from the joint angles. "Forward" = joints → tip. Always has exactly one answer.
Inverse kinematics (IK)
Computing the joint angles from a desired tip position. "Inverse" = tip position → joints. May have multiple answers (elbow-up vs elbow-down) or no answer (target out of reach).
DH parameters
Short for "Denavit-Hartenberg parameters" — a standard recipe of four numbers per joint that describes the geometry of a robot arm. We use a table of DH parameters to build the FK formula automatically.
Jacobian
A mathematical table (matrix) that says "if joint i rotates at speed X, the tip moves at speed Y in each direction." It's the gear ratio for the whole arm at once.
Singularity
A joint configuration where the arm loses the ability to move in one direction no matter how fast the joints spin. Like trying to steer a bike when the handlebars are completely folded — you're stuck.
Matrix
A rectangular grid of numbers. We use matrices to represent rotations and movements in a compact form. Multiplying two matrices is like combining two movements into one.

2.1 Coordinate Frames

A coordinate frame (also called a reference frame) is a set of three mutually perpendicular axes — \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) — attached to a physical point in space. Every measurement (a position, a velocity, a force) is only meaningful relative to some frame. When you say "the end-effector is 0.5 m in front of the robot," the word "in front" implicitly refers to the robot's base frame.

For a robot arm, we attach a coordinate frame to each link. The pose (position + orientation) of frame \(i\) relative to frame \(i-1\) depends on joint angle \(q_i\). Composing all these relative transforms gives the pose of the end-effector in the world frame — this is precisely what forward kinematics computes.

2.1.1 Homogeneous Transforms

A homogeneous transform \(T \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}\) encodes both a rotation and a translation in a single matrix:

\[ T = \begin{bmatrix} R & \mathbf{p} \\ \mathbf{0}^\top & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad R \in SO(3),\quad \mathbf{p} \in \mathbb{R}^3 \]

where \(R\) is a \(3\times3\) rotation matrix and \(\mathbf{p}\) is a translation vector. The advantage of this representation is that composing two transforms is simply matrix multiplication:

\[ T_{0 \to 2} = T_{0 \to 1} \cdot T_{1 \to 2} \]

In plain English: "The transformation from frame 0 to frame 2 equals the transformation from frame 0 to frame 1, chained with the transformation from frame 1 to frame 2." Think of it like directions: (A→C) = (A→B) then (B→C).

For the 7-DOF Panda, computing the end-effector pose in the base frame is:

\[ T_{\text{EEF}} = T_1(q_1)\, T_2(q_2)\, T_3(q_3)\, T_4(q_4)\, T_5(q_5)\, T_6(q_6)\, T_7(q_7) \]

In plain English: Chain all 7 joint transforms together (multiply the matrices left to right), one per joint, and the result tells you exactly where the gripper is and which way it's pointing. Each Ti(qi) depends only on that joint's current angle.

Each \(T_i(q_i)\) is a \(4\times4\) matrix that depends on one joint angle. The Denavit-Hartenberg (DH) convention gives us a systematic way to construct each \(T_i\).

2.2 Denavit-Hartenberg Parameters

The Denavit-Hartenberg (DH) convention [1] is a standardized procedure for assigning coordinate frames to robot links. It represents the transform between consecutive frames using just four parameters per joint:

ParameterSymbolMeaning
Link length\(a_i\)Distance along \(x_i\) from \(z_{i-1}\) to \(z_i\)
Link twist\(\alpha_i\)Angle from \(z_{i-1}\) to \(z_i\) about \(x_i\)
Link offset\(d_i\)Distance along \(z_{i-1}\) from \(x_{i-1}\) to \(x_i\)
Joint angle\(\theta_i\)Angle from \(x_{i-1}\) to \(x_i\) about \(z_{i-1}\) — this is the variable \(q_i\)
Analogy — DH parameters as driving directions: Instead of giving someone your GPS address, imagine describing how to reach it from a starting point: "Walk 2 meters forward, turn left 90°, walk 1 meter, turn right 45°…" Each step uses only two numbers — a distance and an angle. DH parameters do exactly this for robot links: four numbers per joint describe how to step from one joint's coordinate frame to the next. The whole arm's geometry is captured as a short table of these numbers.

For a revolute joint, \(a_i\), \(\alpha_i\), and \(d_i\) are fixed constants of the mechanism's geometry; only \(\theta_i = q_i\) changes as the joint moves. Figure 2.1 illustrates the four steps for a single joint.

The resulting \(4\times4\) DH transform matrix for joint \(i\) is:

\[ T_i(\theta_i) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_i & -\sin\theta_i\cos\alpha_i & \sin\theta_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\cos\theta_i \\ \sin\theta_i & \cos\theta_i\cos\alpha_i & -\cos\theta_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\sin\theta_i \\ 0 & \sin\alpha_i & \cos\alpha_i & d_i \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

2.3 Forward Kinematics

Analogy — FK and IK as two directions of the same puzzle: Hold out your arm and move your shoulder 45° and your elbow 30°. You can work out exactly where your fingertip ends up — that's forward kinematics: given joint angles → find tip position. Now try the reverse: someone places a doorknob at a specific point in space and asks you to touch it. You have to figure out where to angle your shoulder, elbow, and wrist. That's inverse kinematics — it's much harder because there are often multiple solutions (you can reach the same point with your elbow high or low), and sometimes no solution at all (the point is out of reach).

Forward kinematics (FK) is the function \(f: \mathbb{R}^7 \to SE(3)\) that maps a vector of joint angles \(\mathbf{q} = [q_1, \ldots, q_7]^\top\) to the end-effector pose \(T_{\text{EEF}} \in SE(3)\). It is always unique — one joint configuration produces exactly one end-effector pose.

\[ T_{\text{EEF}}(\mathbf{q}) = T_1(q_1)\, T_2(q_2)\, T_3(q_3)\, T_4(q_4)\, T_5(q_5)\, T_6(q_6)\, T_7(q_7)\, T_{\text{EEF}} \]

The result \(T_{\text{EEF}}\) is a \(4\times4\) matrix whose top-left \(3\times3\) block is the end-effector orientation and whose top-right \(3\times1\) column is its position in the robot's base frame.

2.4 Inverse Kinematics

Inverse kinematics (IK) is the reverse problem: given a desired end-effector pose \(T_{\text{des}}\), find joint angles \(\mathbf{q}\) such that \(T_{\text{EEF}}(\mathbf{q}) = T_{\text{des}}\). This is significantly harder than FK for three reasons:

  • Multiple solutions. For most target poses, several different joint configurations produce the same end-effector pose (e.g., elbow-up vs. elbow-down).
  • No solution. A target outside the workspace has no solution.
  • Infinite solutions (redundancy). The Panda has 7 DOF but only 6 are needed to specify a pose. The extra DOF means there is a continuous one-dimensional family of solutions — the null space.

2.4.1 Analytical vs. Numerical IK

MethodHow it worksSpeedUsed in MoveIt 2
Analytical Closed-form algebra (possible for specific arm geometries) Microseconds TRAC-IK (partially analytical)
Numerical (Jacobian) Iterative gradient descent from a seed configuration Milliseconds KDL, TRAC-IK fallback

MoveIt 2 uses TRAC-IK [3] by default for the Panda — a hybrid solver that attempts an analytical solution first and falls back to numerical optimization. In practice, for your Python code, you never call the IK solver directly: you give MoveIt 2 a desired pose and it solves IK internally before planning.

2.4.2 The Null Space

Because the Panda has 7 DOF, there is one extra degree of freedom beyond what is needed to fix the end-effector pose. This manifests as a null-space motion: the arm can move its joints (e.g., rotating the elbow around the shoulder-wrist axis) while keeping the end-effector perfectly stationary. This is useful for:

  • Avoiding joint limits
  • Avoiding obstacles while the EEF stays on a Cartesian path
  • Maintaining a preferred elbow posture
Note: MoveIt 2 automatically exploits the null space during motion planning to avoid obstacles and joint limits. Chapter 8 shows how to add explicit constraints that further direct null-space motion.

2.5 The Jacobian

Analogy — the Jacobian as a bicycle gear system: When you pedal a bicycle, the gear ratio translates your pedaling speed into the wheel's speed. If you are in a very low gear, pedaling fast barely moves the wheel; in a high gear, one pedal revolution sends you far. The Jacobian is the "gear ratio" for a robot arm: it translates how fast each joint is spinning into how fast the tip moves in 3D space. Near a singularity, the Jacobian is like being stuck in neutral — even spinning joints at full speed produces no useful tip motion in one direction.

The Jacobian matrix \(J(\mathbf{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 7}\) is the linearized relationship between joint velocities \(\dot{\mathbf{q}}\) and end-effector velocities:

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} \dot{\mathbf{p}} \\ \boldsymbol{\omega} \end{bmatrix} = J(\mathbf{q})\, \dot{\mathbf{q}} \]

In plain English: "Gripper velocity = Jacobian × joint speeds." The left side v is a 6-number list: the first 3 numbers are how fast the gripper moves in the x, y, z directions (in m/s), and the last 3 are how fast it rotates about those axes (in rad/s). The right side multiplies the Jacobian (a 6×7 table that changes with joint angles) by the 7 joint angular speeds. If you want the gripper to move at a specific velocity, rearrange the equation to find the joint speeds that produce it.

where \(\dot{\mathbf{p}} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the linear velocity of the end-effector and \(\boldsymbol{\omega} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is its angular velocity. The Jacobian depends on the current joint configuration \(\mathbf{q}\) and must be recomputed whenever the arm moves.

The Jacobian is central to three important applications:

  1. Velocity control (Chapter 6) — convert a desired EEF velocity into joint velocity commands: \(\dot{\mathbf{q}} = J^+\mathbf{v}\) where \(J^+\) is the pseudoinverse.
  2. Impedance control (Chapter 7) — map Cartesian forces/torques to joint torques: \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = J^\top \mathbf{F}\).
  3. Singularity detection — the arm is at a singularity when \(\text{rank}(J) < 6\), i.e., when the Jacobian loses rank.

2.6 Workspace and Singularities

2.6.1 Workspace

The workspace is the set of all end-effector positions reachable by the arm. For the Panda, it is approximately a sphere of radius 855 mm centered at the base, with a dead zone near the base where the arm cannot reach itself. Figure 2.4 shows the cross-sectional envelope.

2.6.2 Singularities

A singularity is a joint configuration where the Jacobian loses rank — meaning the arm loses the ability to move in one or more Cartesian directions, no matter how fast the joints spin. Mathematically, this occurs when \(\det(J J^\top) = 0\). Figure 2.5 illustrates the two most common singularity types for the Panda.

Warning: Commanding a robot through a singularity causes joint velocities to spike toward infinity in theory. In practice, the Panda's reflex system will trigger a protective stop. Always check that planned trajectories avoid singular configurations — MoveIt 2's planners do this by default.

2.7 Code Examples

2.7.1 Manual Forward Kinematics

The following script computes the Panda's end-effector pose for a given joint configuration by multiplying the seven DH transform matrices explicitly using NumPy. Running it confirms your understanding of FK before relying on MoveIt 2's internal solver.

fk_manual.py
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
Compute Panda forward kinematics manually using Denavit-Hartenberg parameters.
No ROS or MoveIt required — pure NumPy.

Usage:
    python3 fk_manual.py

Output:
    4x4 end-effector transform in the base frame, plus position and RPY angles.
"""

import numpy as np

# ── Panda DH parameters: (a, d, alpha) per joint ──────────────────────────────
# Source: Franka Robotics technical documentation [2]
# Columns: a_i (m),  d_i (m),  alpha_i (rad)
DH = np.array([
    [0,       0.333,   0         ],   # Joint 1
    [0,       0,      -np.pi/2   ],   # Joint 2
    [0,       0.316,   np.pi/2   ],   # Joint 3
    [0.0825,  0,       np.pi/2   ],   # Joint 4
    [-0.0825, 0.384,  -np.pi/2   ],   # Joint 5
    [0,       0,       np.pi/2   ],   # Joint 6
    [0.088,   0,       np.pi/2   ],   # Joint 7
    [0,       0.107,   0         ],   # EEF (fixed, theta=0)
])


def dh_transform(a: float, d: float, alpha: float, theta: float) -> np.ndarray:
    """Return the 4x4 homogeneous DH transform matrix for one joint."""
    ct, st = np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)
    ca, sa = np.cos(alpha), np.sin(alpha)
    return np.array([
        [ct,  -st*ca,   st*sa,  a*ct],
        [st,   ct*ca,  -ct*sa,  a*st],
        [0,    sa,      ca,     d   ],
        [0,    0,       0,      1   ],
    ])


def forward_kinematics(q: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """
    Compute the end-effector pose given a 7-element joint angle vector.

    Args:
        q: joint angles [q1..q7] in radians

    Returns:
        T_eef: 4x4 homogeneous transform of the end-effector in base frame
    """
    if q.shape != (7,):
        raise ValueError(f"Expected 7 joint angles, got {q.shape}")

    T = np.eye(4)
    for i in range(7):
        a, d, alpha = DH[i]
        T = T @ dh_transform(a, d, alpha, q[i])

    # Fixed end-effector offset (theta = 0)
    a, d, alpha = DH[7]
    T = T @ dh_transform(a, d, alpha, theta=0.0)
    return T


def rotation_to_rpy(R: np.ndarray) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Extract roll-pitch-yaw (XYZ convention) from a 3x3 rotation matrix."""
    pitch = np.arctan2(-R[2, 0], np.sqrt(R[0, 0]**2 + R[1, 0]**2))
    if np.abs(np.cos(pitch)) < 1e-10:
        roll  = 0.0
        yaw   = np.arctan2(-R[1, 2], R[1, 1])
    else:
        roll  = np.arctan2(R[2, 1] / np.cos(pitch), R[2, 2] / np.cos(pitch))
        yaw   = np.arctan2(R[1, 0] / np.cos(pitch), R[0, 0] / np.cos(pitch))
    return roll, pitch, yaw


def main():
    # Panda "ready" pose (approximate, used in many MoveIt demos)
    q_ready = np.array([0, -np.pi/4, 0, -3*np.pi/4, 0, np.pi/2, np.pi/4])

    T = forward_kinematics(q_ready)

    print("=" * 55)
    print("Panda Forward Kinematics — 'ready' configuration")
    print("=" * 55)
    print("\nJoint angles (deg):")
    print("  ", np.round(np.degrees(q_ready), 2))

    print("\nEnd-effector transform T_EEF (4x4):")
    for row in T:
        print("  ", np.round(row, 5))

    position = T[:3, 3]
    roll, pitch, yaw = rotation_to_rpy(T[:3, :3])
    print(f"\nPosition (m):  x={position[0]:.4f}  y={position[1]:.4f}  z={position[2]:.4f}")
    print(f"Orientation:   roll={np.degrees(roll):.2f}°  "
          f"pitch={np.degrees(pitch):.2f}°  yaw={np.degrees(yaw):.2f}°")

    # Sanity check: all-zeros joint angles
    q_zero = np.zeros(7)
    T_zero = forward_kinematics(q_zero)
    print(f"\nAll-zeros config EEF position: {np.round(T_zero[:3,3], 4)}")
    print("  (should be directly above the base, ~0.915 m up)")


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()

2.7.2 Numerical Jacobian Estimation

This script estimates the Panda's geometric Jacobian numerically using finite differences. It is useful for verifying analytical Jacobian implementations and for understanding which joints contribute most to end-effector motion at a given configuration. The script also computes the manipulability — a single number \(w = \sqrt{\det(J J^\top)}\) that summarises how freely the end-effector can move in all directions from the current configuration. A value of zero means the arm is at a singularity; larger values mean more dexterous motion is available [4].

jacobian_numerical.py
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
Numerically estimate the Panda geometric Jacobian via finite differences.

The geometric Jacobian J(q) ∈ R^{6x7} satisfies:
    [p_dot; omega] = J(q) @ q_dot

We estimate it column-by-column: perturb q_i by +/- eps, observe the change
in the FK result, and divide by 2*eps (central difference).

Usage:
    python3 jacobian_numerical.py
"""

import numpy as np
from fk_manual import forward_kinematics


def rotation_to_axis_angle_velocity(R_a: np.ndarray, R_b: np.ndarray,
                                     eps: float) -> np.ndarray:
    """
    Approximate angular velocity omega from two nearby rotation matrices.
    Uses the skew-symmetric part of (R_b @ R_a^T) / (2*eps).
    """
    dR = (R_b @ R_a.T - R_a @ R_b.T) / (4 * eps)
    # Extract [omega_x, omega_y, omega_z] from skew-symmetric dR
    return np.array([dR[2, 1], dR[0, 2], dR[1, 0]])


def jacobian_numerical(q: np.ndarray, eps: float = 1e-6) -> np.ndarray:
    """
    Compute the 6x7 geometric Jacobian at configuration q via central differences.

    Args:
        q:   joint angles, shape (7,)
        eps: finite difference step size (radians)

    Returns:
        J: (6, 7) Jacobian matrix
          Rows 0-2: linear velocity (dp/dq)
          Rows 3-5: angular velocity (domega/dq)
    """
    J = np.zeros((6, 7))
    T0 = forward_kinematics(q)

    for i in range(7):
        q_plus  = q.copy(); q_plus[i]  += eps
        q_minus = q.copy(); q_minus[i] -= eps

        T_plus  = forward_kinematics(q_plus)
        T_minus = forward_kinematics(q_minus)

        # Linear velocity column: central difference of position
        J[:3, i] = (T_plus[:3, 3] - T_minus[:3, 3]) / (2 * eps)

        # Angular velocity column: from rotation matrix change
        J[3:, i] = rotation_to_axis_angle_velocity(
            T_minus[:3, :3], T_plus[:3, :3], eps
        )

    return J


def manipulability(J: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """
    Yoshikawa manipulability measure: sqrt(det(J J^T)).
    Zero at singularities, larger = more dexterous.
    """
    return float(np.sqrt(max(0, np.linalg.det(J @ J.T))))


def main():
    q_ready = np.array([0, -np.pi/4, 0, -3*np.pi/4, 0, np.pi/2, np.pi/4])

    J = jacobian_numerical(q_ready)

    print("=" * 60)
    print("Panda Geometric Jacobian at 'ready' configuration")
    print("=" * 60)
    print("\nJ (6x7) — rows: [vx, vy, vz, wx, wy, wz], cols: joints 1-7")
    header = "       " + "  ".join(f"  J{i+1}   " for i in range(7))
    print(header)
    row_labels = ["vx", "vy", "vz", "wx", "wy", "wz"]
    for row_label, row in zip(row_labels, J):
        formatted = "  ".join(f"{v:+.4f}" for v in row)
        print(f"  {row_label}:  {formatted}")

    m = manipulability(J)
    print(f"\nYoshikawa manipulability:  {m:.6f}")
    print("  (0 = singular, larger = more dexterous)")

    # Demonstrate near-singular configuration
    q_singular = np.array([0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0])  # all zeros → near shoulder sing.
    J_sing = jacobian_numerical(q_singular)
    m_sing = manipulability(J_sing)
    print(f"\nManipulability at all-zeros config: {m_sing:.6f}")
    print("  (closer to 0 → near singularity)")


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
Run both scripts together: Since jacobian_numerical.py imports forward_kinematics from fk_manual.py, both files must be in the same directory. Run python3 fk_manual.py first, then python3 jacobian_numerical.py.
Chapter 2 Summary — What you learned:
  • Coordinate frames are the x/y/z axes attached to each robot link — every position measurement only makes sense relative to a frame.
  • DH parameters give us a 4-number recipe per joint to describe how the geometry steps from one link's frame to the next; for the Panda's 7 joints, this produces a 7-row table.
  • Forward kinematics (FK) is straightforward: multiply 7 DH matrices to get the gripper's exact position and orientation from any set of joint angles.
  • Inverse kinematics (IK) goes the other direction — given a desired gripper pose, find the joint angles that achieve it. Multiple solutions may exist; MoveIt 2 picks the closest one to the current configuration.
  • The Jacobian is the 6×7 "gear ratio" matrix that converts joint speeds into gripper speed; it is also the first tool for detecting singularities (configurations where the arm loses the ability to move in one direction).

Key Terms — Chapter 2

Homogeneous transform
A \(4\times4\) matrix encoding both rotation and translation.
DH parameters
Four numbers \((a,d,\alpha,\theta)\) that define the transform between consecutive joint frames.
Forward kinematics
The map from joint angles to end-effector pose; always unique.
Inverse kinematics
The map from desired pose to joint angles; may have multiple, zero, or infinite solutions.
Jacobian
The \(6\times7\) matrix relating joint velocities to end-effector velocities.
Null space
The set of joint velocities that produce zero end-effector velocity; dimension = 1 for the Panda.
Singularity
A configuration where the Jacobian loses rank; the arm cannot move in certain directions.
Workspace
The set of all end-effector positions reachable by the arm.
Manipulability
A scalar measure of how far the arm is from a singularity; \(\sqrt{\det(JJ^\top)}\).
TRAC-IK
The default inverse kinematics solver used by MoveIt 2 for the Panda.
