Chapter 7

Impedance & Torque Control

Chapters 4–6 told the arm where to go. Impedance control answers a different question: what happens when something pushes the arm? Should it rigidly resist the push? Completely give way? Or spring back gently? You get to choose — by programming the arm's virtual "springiness." This is what allows a robot to work safely next to people: if a human bumps into it, the arm yields instead of pushing back with full motor force.

Key Terms — Chapter 7

Torque
A rotational force — the turning force a motor outputs at a joint. Measured in Newton-meters (N·m).
Impedance
How much the arm resists being pushed off its target position; high impedance = stiff and rigid, low impedance = compliant and yielding.
Impedance control
A control strategy that programs the dynamic relationship between external force and end-effector motion, making the robot behave like a virtual mechanical system.
Stiffness (Kp)
The spring-like restoring force per unit displacement; high stiffness resists deflection, low stiffness yields to contact.
Damping (Kd)
The velocity-dependent resistance that prevents oscillation; critical damping gives the fastest non-oscillatory response.
Compliance
The opposite of stiffness; a compliant arm gives way when pushed, useful for working near people or when accidental contact may occur.
Mass-spring-damper
A standard mechanical model (mass M, spring stiffness K, shock-absorber damping B) that impedance control makes the arm emulate.
Critical damping
The condition \(K_d = 2\sqrt{MK_p}\) at which the system returns to equilibrium as quickly as possible without overshooting.
Wrench
A 6-vector combining a 3D force and a 3D torque — the complete description of the mechanical action on a rigid body.
Jacobian transpose (J^T)
Maps a Cartesian wrench applied at the end-effector to the equivalent joint torques; used in Cartesian impedance control.
Feed-forward torque
The gravity and friction compensation torque computed from the robot's dynamic model so the arm stays at its target pose without position error.
CartesianImpedanceController
The franka_ros2 controller that implements Cartesian-space impedance control with separate stiffness values for each of the 6 Cartesian DOF.
Null-space stiffness
A restoring torque in the redundant null space of the Jacobian that controls elbow configuration independently of end-effector pose.

7.1 What Is Impedance Control?

The position control you used in Chapters 4–5 drives joint angles to a target and fights any external force that opposes the motion — like a rigid robotic arm from a factory. Pure force control, at the other extreme, maintains a target contact force but ignores where the end-effector ends up. Impedance control is a middle ground: it controls the dynamic relationship between force and motion [1]. Think of it as programming the arm's "give."

The intuition is a virtual spring. Imagine a spring connecting the desired pose to the actual pose of the end-effector. When the environment pushes the arm away from its goal, the spring pulls it back — harder if the spring is stiff, more gently if the spring is soft. The beauty of this approach is that you can change the spring stiffness in software at any moment. You choose the spring stiffness \(K_p\) and the damping \(K_d\) to set the compliance of the arm:

Table 7.1 — Effect of stiffness and damping parameters
ParameterLow valueHigh valueTypical use case
\(K_p\) (stiffness) Compliant — arm yields to contact Stiff — resists deflection Low: human interaction, polishing. High: precision insertion.
\(K_d\) (damping) Underdamped — oscillates after disturbance Overdamped — sluggish return Tune to critical damping: \(K_d = 2\sqrt{M K_p}\)

Because torque is commanded directly to the motors, impedance control requires access to the torque interface — available on the real Panda hardware (which you recall has a torque sensor at every joint, as described in Chapter 1) and in the Gazebo simulation through franka_ros2's CartesianImpedanceController. Switching to this controller follows the same lifecycle pattern described in Chapter 3's controller manager section.

7.2 The Mass-Spring-Damper Model

Analogy — the mass-spring-damper as a car suspension: Your car's suspension has three components: the wheel's mass (M), the coil spring (K), and the shock absorber (B). Hit a pothole and the wheel drops fast. The spring pushes it back up; the shock absorber slows that rebound so the car doesn't bounce forever. Impedance control programs the same three-component behavior into the robot arm: M controls how it reacts to sudden forces, K controls how firmly it returns to the target position, and B controls how quickly it settles without oscillating. Tune all three to make the arm feel exactly as stiff or compliant as the task requires.

The mechanical model behind impedance control is a mass \(M\) connected to a fixed wall by a spring with stiffness constant \(K\) and a dashpot with damping constant \(B\) in parallel. An external force \(F_{\mathrm{ext}}\) is applied to the mass. Newton's second law gives the equation of motion:

\[ M\ddot{x} + B\dot{x} + K x = F_{\mathrm{ext}} \]

M = mass (how sluggish the system is), B = damping (how fast oscillations die), K = stiffness (how hard the spring pulls back). Adjust these three numbers to change how the arm feels when you push it.

Here \(x\) is the displacement from the equilibrium position, \(\dot{x}\) is velocity, and \(\ddot{x}\) is acceleration. In impedance control we choose the torque command so that the robot behaves as if it were this mechanical system.

The transfer function from \(F_{\mathrm{ext}}\) to displacement \(x\) in the Laplace domain is the mechanical impedance:

\[ Z(s) = M s^2 + B s + K \]

Impedance measures "how hard is it to push this thing?" High impedance (large M, B, K) = rigid and hard to deflect. Low impedance = compliant and easy to push aside.

A high-impedance system (large \(M\), \(B\), \(K\)) is hard to deflect — the arm pushes back firmly. A low-impedance system yields easily — the arm feels almost weightless when you guide it by hand. Impedance control lets you set \(K\) and \(B\) in software, effectively reprogramming the arm's mechanical personality without touching any hardware.

7.3 The Impedance Control Law

In joint-space impedance control (the form used by franka_ros2), the torque command for each joint is:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = K_p\,(\mathbf{q}_d - \mathbf{q}) + K_d\,(\dot{\mathbf{q}}_d - \dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{ff}} \]

The arm acts like a spring anchored at q_d: displace it and it pushes back. Kp controls spring stiffness; Kd controls how quickly the oscillation dies. τ_ff cancels gravity so the arm holds position without effort.

where \(\mathbf{q}_d\) is the desired joint angle vector, \(\mathbf{q}\) is the current measured joint angle, \(\dot{\mathbf{q}}_d\) is the desired joint velocity, \(\dot{\mathbf{q}}\) is measured joint velocity, and \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is a gravity/friction feed-forward torque computed by the dynamics model [2].

In Cartesian-space impedance control, the spring acts on the pose error in task space. The desired wrench (force + torque) on the end-effector is:

\[ \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{cmd}} = K_p\,(\mathbf{x}_d - \mathbf{x}) + K_d\,(\dot{\mathbf{x}}_d - \dot{\mathbf{x}}) \]

Same spring idea, now in Cartesian space: push the gripper away from x_d and it resists with a force proportional to the distance. Useful for compliant assembly tasks.

This Cartesian wrench is then mapped to joint torques via the transpose Jacobian:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = J(q)^{\top} \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{cmd}} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{ff}} \]

Jᵀ converts the desired Cartesian pushing force into the joint torques that produce it. τ_ff compensates for gravity so the arm does not sag under its own weight.

7.3.1 Gravity and Friction Feed-Forward

Without feed-forward, the arm would drift downward under gravity whenever \(\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{q}_d\) (zero error). The Panda's franka_ros2 driver computes the required gravity and friction torques automatically from the dynamic model and adds them to the impedance command, so the arm stays at the target pose even with \(K_p = 0\).

7.4 Stiffness and Damping Space

For a given virtual mass \(M\), the relationship between \(K_p\) (stiffness) and \(K_d\) (damping) determines whether the system is underdamped, critically damped, or overdamped. The critical damping condition is:

\[ K_d = 2\sqrt{M\,K_p} \]

Below the critical curve the system oscillates after a disturbance; above it the response is sluggish. Figure 7.3 maps the parameter space.

For the Panda, the CartesianImpedanceController accepts separate stiffness values for each of the 6 Cartesian DOF (3 translational + 3 rotational), allowing the arm to be, for example, stiff in the Z direction while compliant in XY — ideal for surface-following tasks.

7.5 Implementation on the Panda

The franka_ros2 package ships a ready-made CartesianImpedanceController. Activate it alongside the joint state broadcaster using the following YAML configuration:

Add the following block to the same controller YAML file used in Chapter 6 (ros2_controllers.yaml).

ros2_controllers.yaml — add this block
cartesian_impedance_controller:
  ros__parameters:
    arm_id: panda
    joints:
      - panda_joint1
      - panda_joint2
      - panda_joint3
      - panda_joint4
      - panda_joint5
      - panda_joint6
      - panda_joint7
    # Translational stiffness (N/m) for x, y, z
    cartesian_stiffness_translation: [300.0, 300.0, 300.0]
    # Rotational stiffness (N·m/rad) for roll, pitch, yaw
    cartesian_stiffness_rotation:    [30.0, 30.0, 30.0]
    # Damping ratio (0=no damping, 1=critical)
    damping_ratio: 1.0

Load the controller, then switch from the trajectory controller. Run these commands in a terminal while the simulation is already running:

bash — terminal with simulation running
ros2 run controller_manager spawner cartesian_impedance_controller --inactive

ros2 control switch_controllers \
    --activate cartesian_impedance_controller \
    --deactivate joint_trajectory_controller
Hardware safety note. The impedance controller bypasses MoveIt 2's collision checking. Always test with very low stiffness (\(K_p < 50\)) and observe the arm before increasing values. Keep the emergency stop button within reach during every impedance control session.

7.5.1 Updating Stiffness at Runtime

The stiffness and damping parameters are exposed as ROS 2 parameters and can be updated at any time without restarting the controller:

bash — update stiffness at runtime (terminal with sim running)
# Set translational stiffness to 500 N/m on all axes
ros2 param set /cartesian_impedance_controller \
    cartesian_stiffness_translation "[500.0, 500.0, 500.0]"

7.6 Code Examples

7.6.1 Compliance Demo — Low-Stiffness Pose Holding

The following node activates the impedance controller at very low stiffness so the arm can be guided by hand. The target pose is updated to track the current pose, giving the sensation of a "gravity-compensated" mode where the arm floats. 

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
compliance_demo.py — run the arm in a highly compliant mode.

The target pose is set to the current pose, so the only restoring force
comes from Kp * 0 = 0. Gravity compensation keeps the arm floating.
Increase Kp to add virtual stiffness around the current configuration.
"""
import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from geometry_msgs.msg import PoseStamped
from sensor_msgs.msg import JointState
import moveit_py
from moveit.core.robot_state import RobotState

KP_LOW    = [10.0, 10.0, 10.0]   # N/m  — very compliant (translation)
KP_HIGH   = [300.0, 300.0, 300.0] # N/m — normal stiffness
KR_LOW    = [2.0, 2.0, 2.0]       # N·m/rad — rotational stiffness
KR_HIGH   = [20.0, 20.0, 20.0]


class ComplianceDemoNode(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('compliance_demo')

        self.moveit = moveit_py.MoveItPy(node_name='compliance_demo')
        robot_model = self.moveit.get_robot_model()
        self.robot_state = RobotState(robot_model)

        self._q = [0.0] * 7
        self.create_subscription(
            JointState, '/joint_states', self._joint_cb, 10)

        self.target_pub = self.create_publisher(
            PoseStamped,
            '/cartesian_impedance_controller/equilibrium_pose',
            10,
        )
        self.get_logger().info(
            'Compliance demo ready. The arm should float freely.'
        )

    def _joint_cb(self, msg: JointState) -> None:
        joint_names = [
            'panda_joint1', 'panda_joint2', 'panda_joint3', 'panda_joint4',
            'panda_joint5', 'panda_joint6', 'panda_joint7',
        ]
        for name, pos in zip(msg.name, msg.position):
            if name in joint_names:
                self._q[joint_names.index(name)] = pos

    def update_target_to_current(self) -> None:
        """Publish the current EEF pose as the new equilibrium target."""
        self.robot_state.set_joint_group_positions('panda_arm', self._q)
        pose = self.robot_state.get_global_link_transform('panda_hand')

        msg = PoseStamped()
        msg.header.stamp = self.get_clock().now().to_msg()
        msg.header.frame_id = 'panda_link0'
        msg.pose.position.x = float(pose[0, 3])
        msg.pose.position.y = float(pose[1, 3])
        msg.pose.position.z = float(pose[2, 3])
        # Identity orientation quaternion (not rotating equilibrium)
        msg.pose.orientation.w = 1.0
        self.target_pub.publish(msg)


def main():
    rclpy.init()
    node = ComplianceDemoNode()

    # Run for 30 seconds; the arm can be pushed by hand
    import time
    t_end = time.time() + 30.0
    while rclpy.ok() and time.time() < t_end:
        rclpy.spin_once(node, timeout_sec=0.05)
        node.update_target_to_current()

    node.destroy_node()
    rclpy.shutdown()


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()

7.6.2 Joint-Space Impedance Controller (Torque Interface)

For full control over joint torques, the example below computes torques directly from the impedance law and publishes them to the joint torque interface. 

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
joint_impedance.py — direct joint-space impedance via the torque interface.

Publishes tau = Kp*(q_d - q) + Kd*(qdot_d - qdot) + tau_gravity
at 1 kHz to /effort_controllers/commands.
"""
import numpy as np
import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from std_msgs.msg import Float64MultiArray
from sensor_msgs.msg import JointState

JOINT_NAMES = [
    'panda_joint1', 'panda_joint2', 'panda_joint3', 'panda_joint4',
    'panda_joint5', 'panda_joint6', 'panda_joint7',
]
KP = np.array([600., 600., 600., 600., 250., 150., 50.])  # N·m/rad
KD = np.array([ 50.,  50.,  50.,  20.,  20.,  20., 10.])  # N·m·s/rad


class JointImpedanceNode(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('joint_impedance')

        self.tau_pub = self.create_publisher(
            Float64MultiArray, '/effort_controllers/commands', 10)

        self._q    = np.zeros(7)
        self._qdot = np.zeros(7)
        self.create_subscription(
            JointState, '/joint_states', self._joint_cb, 10)

    def _joint_cb(self, msg: JointState) -> None:
        for name, pos, vel in zip(msg.name, msg.position, msg.velocity):
            if name in JOINT_NAMES:
                i = JOINT_NAMES.index(name)
                self._q[i]    = pos
                self._qdot[i] = vel

    def send_torque(self, q_d: np.ndarray, qdot_d: np.ndarray) -> None:
        """Compute and publish the impedance torque command."""
        tau = KP * (q_d - self._q) + KD * (qdot_d - self._qdot)
        msg = Float64MultiArray()
        msg.data = tau.tolist()
        self.tau_pub.publish(msg)


def main():
    rclpy.init()
    node = JointImpedanceNode()
    rclpy.spin_once(node, timeout_sec=0.5)  # receive one joint state

    # Hold the current pose for 10 seconds
    import time
    q_hold = node._q.copy()
    dt     = 0.001   # 1 kHz
    t_end  = time.time() + 10.0
    while rclpy.ok() and time.time() < t_end:
        rclpy.spin_once(node, timeout_sec=0)
        node.send_torque(q_d=q_hold, qdot_d=np.zeros(7))
        time.sleep(dt)

    node.destroy_node()
    rclpy.shutdown()


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
Chapter 7 Summary
  • Impedance control gives the arm a programmable "feel." Instead of commanding exact positions, you define stiffness (Kp) and damping (Kd), and the controller computes torques that make the arm behave like a spring-damper system. A purely position-controlled arm would snap a human's hand or break a fragile part by ignoring contact; an impedance-controlled arm deflects gracefully. This is what makes safe human–robot collaboration possible.
  • The design target is the mass–spring–damper equation M·ẍ + B·ẋ + K·x = Fext. The arm doesn't physically change — but the controller computes torques that make it respond as if it were a spring with stiffness K and damper with coefficient B. You tune those numbers to trade off precision against compliance.
  • Tuning rule: set Kd ≈ 2√(M·Kp) for a critically damped response — the arm settles quickly without oscillating. Too little Kd and the arm bounces; too much and it responds sluggishly to disturbances.
  • Impedance control requires the torque interface — not the position interface used in Chapters 4–5. On real Panda hardware this is available through franka_ros2's CartesianImpedanceController. This is a harder interface to use safely: a wrong torque command can cause sudden, large joint motion.

Exercises

  1. Set \(K_p = 0\) and \(K_d = 50\,\mathrm{N{\cdot}s/m}\) in joint_impedance.py. What does the arm feel like under this configuration? This mode is called pure damping or rate damping.
  2. Derive the critical damping condition \(K_d = 2\sqrt{M K_p}\) from the characteristic equation of the second-order system \(M\ddot{x} + K_d\dot{x} + K_p x = 0\). (Hint: set the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial to zero.)
  3. Modify compliance_demo.py to hold a fixed target pose instead of tracking the current pose, and gradually increase \(K_p\) from 10 to 300. Observe how the arm's resistance to hand-guiding changes with stiffness.
  4. The Panda supports null-space stiffness — a restoring torque that keeps the elbow in a preferred configuration while the end-effector stays at its target. Look up nullspace_stiffness in the franka_ros2 documentation and add it to the compliance demo.
